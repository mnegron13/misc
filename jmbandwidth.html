What it adds up to

The expansion doesn't add load

Both roles land at roughly the same weekly commitment. Trading one Table Read for dedicated trainings across three teams reshuffles the work more than it grows it, because the new team-specific work is modest and the general monthly wrap shrinks as those teams peel off to their own content. If that surprises you, the three-team build estimates are the likeliest place they run low.

Meetings were the missing piece

Adding meetings closes most of the gap this view kept flagging. A calendar sample puts Jeffrey at roughly 6 hours of meetings a week (1:1s, syncs, All-Hands, planning), separate from shrinkage, which lifts the committed load to about 60% of the working week. What's left is a mix of the undercount noted below and genuine room. Real work on the plate is what tells you which.

These are Jeffrey's numbers, and they hold together

The current-role figures are Jeffrey's own, and the Table Read breakdown lands right on the ~4 h/week the v2.5 time-savings doc used for the same work, a good sign they're trustworthy. The few new-role lines marked est are still estimates awaiting Jeffrey's input.

Staying current on the product is the swing factor

At 4 h/week it's the biggest task line after meetings, and it carries into both roles unchanged since it doesn't scale with team count. If that number is soft, the whole picture moves with it, so it's the first one worth pressure-testing.

Read these as heads-down hours

They're focused task times, not elapsed calendar time. Real weeks carry interruptions and context-switching these don't capture, so true utilization is probably somewhat higher than the percentages show. Not near full, though. The gap is too wide for that to be the whole story.

What to do next

Test the ceiling with real work. Hand Jeffrey a side project or two to run alongside the core role, and watch whether both can be carried at once. The time the new model buys back should leave room for it, and the test reveals where the real limit sits, which the numbers alone can't.
Push from 60 toward 70. With meetings counted, the week already sits near 60%. The room that's left is the target for a side project or two, deliberately loading toward the top of a healthy range while keeping a buffer rather than running to the edge.
Let quality be the signal. Treat it as a genuine test: if the core role or the standard of the work starts to slip, that's the true ceiling, not the percentage on the dial.
